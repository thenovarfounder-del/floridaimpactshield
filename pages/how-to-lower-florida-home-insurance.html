How to Lower Your Florida Home Insurance: The Complete 2026 Playbook

Most advice about lowering home insurance was written for states where a hurricane is a rumor. In Florida the math is different, the credits are different, and the biggest lever is one most homeowners never claim. Here is the playbook ranked by what actually moves your premium.

The heavy hitters

1. Claim every wind mitigation credit. Florida law requires insurers to discount premiums for construction features that reduce wind damage. A certified inspector documents them on a standard form, and your carrier applies credits for what is verified. If your inspection is expired or was never done, you may be paying for credits you already qualify for.

2. Add opening protection. This is typically the largest single wind mitigation credit available. It requires impact-rated windows and doors on every opening — partial coverage usually earns nothing, because one unprotected opening is all a storm needs. This is the only major credit you can earn without touching your roof.

3. Address roof age and attachment. Roof covering, deck attachment, roof-to-wall attachment and roof geometry all carry credits, and many carriers now decline or surcharge older roofs outright. If your roof is nearing replacement, timing it to capture the credits is worth planning.

The moderate levers

4. Raise your deductible if you hold the reserves to absorb it — and understand Florida hurricane deductibles are percentage-based, so a 2 percent deductible on a $400,000 home is $8,000 out of pocket before coverage responds.

5. Audit your dwelling coverage. Coverage should reflect the cost to rebuild, not market value or purchase price. Homeowners are frequently over-insured relative to actual replacement cost.

6. Shop the market annually. Carriers price identical homes very differently, and loyalty is not rewarded in this market. An independent agent can quote multiple carriers at once.

7. Bundle auto and home where the combined price actually beats separate best-in-class policies — check the math rather than assuming.

The smaller adjustments

8. Security and safety systems — monitored alarms, water leak detection and similar devices carry modest credits with many carriers.

9. Pay annually rather than monthly where installment fees apply.

10. Improve your insurance score where it is used, since it factors into pricing in many cases.

11. Ask directly what discounts exist. Carriers do not volunteer every available credit, and agents apply what they are told about. Senior discounts, new-buyer credits and claims-free credits often go unclaimed.

Putting them in order

If you do one thing, get a current wind mitigation inspection — it costs relatively little and can reveal credits you already qualify for. If you do two, price opening protection for your home, because it is the largest credit and it compounds with energy savings, noise reduction and storm safety.

The rest are worth doing, but they trim. The wind mitigation credits restructure what you pay for the life of the home.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fastest way to lower my Florida home insurance?

Getting a current wind mitigation inspection and confirming every earned credit is applied is usually the fastest, since it can reveal discounts you already qualify for without any construction.

Do impact windows really lower insurance in Florida?

Opening protection is typically the largest wind mitigation credit available on a Florida policy. Actual savings vary by carrier, county and home, but it is the most significant homeowner-controlled discount.

Is it worth raising my hurricane deductible?

Only if you can comfortably absorb the out-of-pocket amount. Florida hurricane deductibles are percentage-based, so a 2 percent deductible on a $400,000 home means $8,000 before coverage applies.

Should I shop for insurance every year in Florida?

Yes. Carriers reprice frequently and differently, and renewal loyalty is not rewarded. An independent agent quoting multiple carriers annually is standard practice for Florida homeowners.
