Why Did My Florida Home Insurance Go Up? The Real Reasons Explained

Opening your renewal notice in Florida has become an annual exercise in disbelief. Nothing about your house changed, you filed no claims, and the number went up anyway. The reasons are structural, and understanding them matters — because a few of the factors driving your bill are outside your control, and a few are entirely within it.

The forces pushing Florida rates up

Florida sits in the most hurricane-exposed insurance market in the country, and carriers price accordingly. When a major storm makes landfall anywhere in the state, losses ripple across every policyholder, not just those in the damage path.

Reinsurance is the piece most homeowners never hear about. Your insurer buys its own insurance from global reinsurers, and when those costs rise — driven by worldwide catastrophe losses, not just Florida ones — the increase flows straight through to your premium. Reinsurance has been one of the single largest cost drivers in the Florida market.

Litigation costs have historically been a major factor as well. Florida generated a disproportionate share of the nation’s property insurance lawsuits relative to its share of claims, and reform efforts in recent years targeted exactly that imbalance. Whether those reforms translate into consumer savings has been the central question in the market.

Then simple economics: rebuilding costs more than it used to. Materials, labor and the cost to make you whole after a loss have all climbed, and insurers price the replacement cost of your home, not what you paid for it.

Why your specific policy went up

Beyond market-wide pressure, individual factors matter: the age of your roof (many carriers now impose age limits or require inspections), your home’s construction era, your county, your claims history, your credit-based insurance score in states where it applies, and whether your carrier is re-underwriting its book to shed risk.

One factor catches homeowners repeatedly: an expired wind mitigation inspection. Reports are generally valid for five years, and when yours lapses without renewal, credits you were receiving can quietly disappear from your policy at the next renewal.

What you actually control

You cannot fix reinsurance markets or Florida’s exposure. You can control what your home documents to the carrier, and that is where real money lives.

The wind mitigation credits are the largest homeowner-controlled lever on a Florida policy, and opening protection is typically the biggest single credit within them. Impact-rated windows and doors on every opening move you from the least-credited category to the most-credited one.

The other controllable levers: raising your deductible if you have the reserves to absorb it, bundling policies, verifying your dwelling coverage is not inflated beyond actual replacement cost, refreshing an expired wind mitigation inspection, and shopping the market rather than auto-renewing — carriers price the same home very differently.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Florida home insurance so expensive?

Hurricane exposure, reinsurance costs, historically high litigation volume, and rising rebuilding costs combine to make Florida the most expensive property insurance market in the country. Most of these are market-wide forces rather than anything about your individual home.

My premium went up but I had no claims. Why?

Rate increases are usually driven by market-wide costs, not your personal claims history. Carrier re-underwriting, roof age thresholds, and expired wind mitigation reports also commonly cause increases on claim-free policies.

What is the single biggest discount available on a Florida policy?

Wind mitigation credits, and within them opening protection is typically the largest. It requires impact-rated protection on every opening, verified by a certified inspection.

Can I do anything to lower my premium this year?

Yes: refresh an expired wind mitigation inspection, verify your dwelling coverage matches actual replacement cost, consider a higher deductible, shop multiple carriers, and pursue opening protection for the largest structural credit.
